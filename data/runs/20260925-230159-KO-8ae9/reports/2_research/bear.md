
Bear Analyst: **Bull, I’ll give you credit: Q2’26 was a good quarter.** Revenue accelerated, EPS jumped, and the balance sheet is genuinely healthier than it was a year ago. At **~$87.80**, KO trades at **26.4x TTM earnings, 24.9x forward, a 4.0 PEG, 10.45x book, and a 2.41% yield**—below its historical ~3% average. That is not a market pricing a slow grower. That is a market already pricing the inflection you claim it’s ignoring.

Let me take your points head-on.

---

### 1. The “growth reacceleration” is not proven—and the EPS quality is weaker than you suggest

Yes, Q2’26 revenue grew **+6.7% YoY** and diluted EPS grew **+17% YoY**. But look underneath the EPS number:

- Q2’26 **operating income** was **$4.695B vs $4.351B**—only **+7.9% YoY**.
- Q2’26 **operating margin** was **35.1% vs 34.7%**—just **40bps** of expansion.
- The 17% EPS growth was flattered by **lower interest expense** (-17% YoY) and a **lower tax rate** (Q2’26 net margin 33.1% vs 30.4%). That is not durable operating leverage. That’s below-the-line help.

You say growth is price/mix-led, which is “higher quality.” I say price/mix-led growth without volume is exactly what you’d expect from a mature staples company squeezing its consumers. It is vulnerable to **GLP-1 adoption, sugar taxes, health-conscious trade-down, and private-label competition**. One quarter of +6.7% revenue does not erase a **FY22–FY25 revenue CAGR of ~3.7%**. KO is still a low-single-digit grower at its core.

---

### 2. Your forward PEG argument is a dodge

You claim the 4.0 PEG collapses to ~1.5 if forward EPS grows 16%. But PEG is not meant to use a single-year rebound. It uses **sustainable long-term growth**. KO’s long-term algorithm is mid-single-digit revenue and high-single-digit EPS at best. At **24.9x forward earnings**, that’s a PEG of roughly **3–4x**, not 1.5x.

And your own target math proves my point: **25x forward EPS of $3.53 = $88.25**—essentially today’s price. That’s not a bull argument. That’s an admission that the stock is already trading at your base-case fair value. To get to $95, you need **27x**; to get $105, you need **30x**. For a consumer staples company with a 2.4% yield, that’s multiple expansion on top of an already premium multiple. That’s a fragile thesis.

The 2.41% yield is the tell. Income investors are paying up for a below-average yield because the price has run. If growth disappoints, the multiple compresses. At **20x forward EPS of $3.53**, KO is **$70.60**—about **20% downside**. That aligns with the 52-week low of **$65.35**.

---

### 3. The cash flow “red flag” is real—and your $14.3B trailing FCF is not the clean number you claim

You argue FY25 FCF of **$5.30B** vs dividends of **$8.78B** was just working-capital timing. Fine. But that’s exactly the problem: **KO’s cash flow is volatile and unreliable.** The IRS deposit, payables swings, and prepaid asset movements are enormous. FY25 FCF payout was **166%**. That is not a one-off footnote; it’s a warning that reported FCF can swing violently.

You then say trailing four-quarter FCF is **~$14.3B**, making the FCF yield ~3.8%. But that trailing figure **excludes the massive Q1’25 outflow** that drove FY25 FCF down to $5.3B. You’re cherry-picking a window that flatters the number. Even if we accept $14.3B, a **3.8% FCF yield** on a **25x P/E** stock with a **2.4% dividend yield** is not compelling. And if working capital reverses, that yield evaporates.

Finally, the “stale vendor FCF field” argument is weak. Institutional investors don’t misprice a $378B mega-cap because of one vendor’s data artifact. The market sees the same cash flow volatility. That’s why the stock isn’t cheaper.

### 4. The balance sheet is better—but it’s not a game-changer

I’ll grant you the deleveraging: net debt down **$9.2B YoY**, tangible book value swinging from **-$6.58B to +$8.19B**, and interest coverage at **~12x**. That’s real improvement.

But context matters:

- **Gross debt is still $43.5B.**
- **Debt/Equity is 1.16x.**
- **Tangible book value of $8.19B is only ~2% of the $377.8B market cap.**
- Much of the improvement came from **divestitures and FX translation**, not organic cash generation.

The IRS transfer-pricing dispute remains unresolved. The **$12.33B cumulative FX translation loss** is still a persistent headwind. Equity income from bottling partners ($384M–$604M per quarter) is lumpy. This is not a fortress balance sheet; it’s a leveraged staple that has been repairing itself.
