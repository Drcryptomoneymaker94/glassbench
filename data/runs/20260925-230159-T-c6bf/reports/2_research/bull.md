
Bull Analyst: # Bull Case for AT&T (`T`): The Market Is Mispricing a Cash Machine

Alright, let me set the table before the bear gets a word in. The bear's entire thesis is going to rest on three pillars: **leverage, interest expense, and slow growth.** I'm going to show you why each one is either already priced in, structurally manageable, or flat-out misread — and why the asymmetry here favors the bulls at ~$25.

---

## 1. The Growth Story Isn't Revenue — It's the Mix

The bear loves to say "telecom doesn't grow." Guilty as charged on headline revenue — Q2'26 came in at $31.56B, up **2.3% YoY**. But that's the wrong number to stare at.

Look at what's actually compounding underneath:

- **Operating margin hit 23.2% in Q2'26** — up ~210 bps YoY and the best of the last five quarters. Gross margin expanded to **61.8%**. This is a management team that has cut non-core drag (WarnerMedia spun, DIRECTV stake reduced) and is running a leaner connectivity pure-play.
- **Share count is collapsing ~4% a year** — 7,219M diluted shares in Q2'25 → **6,946M in Q2'26**. That means even *flat* net income grows EPS ~4% mechanically, before any operational improvement.

Stack it up: 2% revenue + 200 bps margin expansion + 4% buyback shrink = **mid-to-high single-digit EPS growth from a company the market is valuing at under 10x forward earnings.** That's not a value trap. That's a compounding machine wearing a value-stock suit.

**And the forward-looking growth levers are real:** fiber expansion drives higher-ARPU, lower-churn customers; convergence bundling (wireless + fiber on one bill) is the single best churn-reduction tool in telecom; and the 5G buildout positions AT&T to capture enterprise and fixed-wireless demand. This isn't speculative — it's showing up in the margin line *today*.

## 2. Competitive Advantages the Bear Will Ignore

- **Scale and spectrum.** ~$127B in TTM revenue, one of the largest US wireless footprints, and a deep spectrum position. You cannot replicate this. It's a licensed, regulated moat.
- **Convergence.** AT&T is one of the few players that can bundle mobility + fiber + enterprise connectivity. Customers who bundle churn dramatically less. That's a structural pricing and retention advantage.
- **Divestiture discipline.** The reset from media conglomerate to focused connectivity company was painful, but it worked. Capital is now flowing to the network and to shareholders instead of into a media arms race AT&T was never going to win.
- **A 4.36% dividend with a ~45% FCF payout.** Compare that to AT&T's pre-2022 history when the payout was stretched and the dividend was a liability. Today it's ~45% of FCF and ~43% of forward EPS. **That is a fortress-level, sustainable income stream.**

## 4. Now Let Me Dismantle the Bear Argument Before It's Made

### Bear point #1: "Leverage is at the ceiling — $162.9B gross debt, 2.5x net leverage."

Yes, net debt is $126.4B and net leverage is ~2.5x. But **that is exactly management's stated target, and it's investment-grade.** More importantly, look at coverage: **EBIT/Interest ≈ 4.1x and EBITDA/Interest ≈ 7.6x.** A company covering its interest 7.6x over EBITDA is not a company in distress. This is a normal, healthy capital structure for a regulated telecom with contractual, subscription-like revenue. The bear is calling a stable, targeted leverage ratio a crisis. It isn't.

### Bear point #2: "Interest expense is up 13.8% YoY."

True — $1.883B in Q2'26 vs $1.655B a year ago. But here's the context the bear conveniently omits: **TTM interest is $7.19B against ~$54B in normalized EBITDA.** That's a 13% interest burden on a business with 43% net margins. The rise is driven by the rate environment and the refinancing of older, cheap debt — a headwind, absolutely, but one that's already embedded in the **9.9x forward multiple**, not the 8.4x trailing. We're not pretending it doesn't exist; we're saying the market has already accounted for it and then some.

### Bear point #3: "Buybacks are debt-funded."

This is the bear's strongest point, and I'll engage it honestly: **H1'26 debt issuance of $14.0B exceeded repayments of $5.4B.** But two things defuse the alarm:

1. **FCF covers the capital return with room to spare.** TTM FCF $17.7B vs TTM dividends ~$8B + H1'26 buybacks $4.67B (~$9.3B annualized). That's ~$12.7B of a $17.7B FCF pie — a >25% cushion before you touch a single dollar of debt.
2. **The debt issuance is largely refinancing, not net-new leverage.** Gross debt rose $7.8B over two quarters against a company generating ~$39.9B in operating cash flow. This is balance-sheet management, not reckless borrowing.

And critically — **as buybacks shrink the share count, EPS rises, which supports the equity value against the debt.** This is the opposite of a death spiral; it's an accretion flywheel.

### Bear point #4: "The 8.4x TTM P/E is flattered by a one-time gain."

**I agree — and I'm not using it.** The Q3'25 EPS of $1.29 was inflated by a ~$5.7B one-time investment gain. That's exactly why the honest, normalized multiple is **~9.9x forward.** Here's the thing the bear won't say out loud: **9.9x forward earnings for a 4.36%-yielding, 0.43-beta, $17.7B-FCF business is still deeply cheap.** If the bear's best argument is "the stock trades at 9.9x instead of 8.4x," the bear case is a rounding error, not a thesis.

### Bear point #5: "Capex intensity is ~17.5% of revenue — no FCF inflection."

This is the one where I actually have the most optimism, and here's why: **capex is building an asset, not leaking cash.** Every dollar of fiber capex goes onto the balance sheet as a long-lived revenue-generating asset with 20+ year economics. Compare AT&T's capex intensity to a software company's R&D — it's investment, not expense. And the moment management signals peak capex, the **entire $22.2B annual capex line becomes the FCF catalyst.** A 200–300 bps reduction in capex intensity would add **billions in annual FCF** — money that flows straight to buybacks and deleveraging. The bear sees a cash sink; I see a coiled spring.

### Bear point #6: "Negative tangible book of -$88.6B."

This is a red herring. **Telecom is valued on cash flow, not liquidation value.** The "intangible-heavy" balance sheet is spectrum licenses, goodwill from acquired networks, and customer relationships — assets that produce the $127B revenue stream every year. No telecom is analyzed on tangible book. The bear is applying an industrial-company lens to a subscription business.

### Bear point #7: "No insider buying."

Fair to note, but **the complete absence of insider *selling* matters just as much.** The only open-market sale in 13 months was 1,500 shares — immaterial. Everything else is scheduled compensation and charitable gifts. Insiders aren't fleeing; they're getting granted shares in the low-$20s and holding. That's not a red flag.

## 5. The Asymmetry — Why the Bulls Win

Here's the setup:

- **Downside protection:** 4.36% dividend covered at ~45% of FCF, beta of 0.43, $17.7B in FCF, and a multiple (6.4x EV/EBITDA) already near the low end of its historical range.
- **Upside catalysts:** a capex peak → FCF inflection → accelerated buybacks → re-rating; continued margin expansion; and any improvement in rate sentiment compressing the interest headwind.

At ~$25, near both the 50-day and 200-day averages, the market is offering us a defensive, cash-generating, dividend-paying business at a valuation that prices in the bear case — and leaves the bull case as upside.

The bear will talk about leverage, rates, and slow growth. Those are **knowns, and they're in the price.** What's not fully in the price is the operating momentum (23.2% margins), the share-count flywheel (-4%/yr), and the coming FCF inflection when capex rolls over.
