
Bear Analyst: Bull, you’ve made the best possible case for **T** — but you did it by cherry-picking the flattering numbers and hand-waving the balance sheet. Let’s debate the actual risk, because the stock at ~$25.28 is not a mispriced cash machine. It’s a leveraged, capital-intensive, low-growth telecom with a 4.36% yield and very little margin for error.

## 1. Your “compounding machine” is mostly financial engineering

You’re right that revenue is stable — Q2’26 was $31.56B, up 2.3% YoY. But that’s not growth. That’s basically inflation-level revenue in a saturated market. The EPS story is not operational magic; it’s cost cuts plus buybacks.

And here’s the problem you skip: **forward EPS is $2.57, below TTM reported EPS of $3.03.** The TTM number is flattered by the ~$5.7B one-time Q3’25 investment gain. So the honest multiple is ~9.9x forward, not 8.4x trailing. That’s not “deeply cheap” for a company with 2% revenue growth, $163B of gross debt, and a PEG of 1.54. A PEG above 1 means you’re not getting growth for the valuation.

You call the 4% annual share shrink a flywheel. I call it a treadmill: **H1’26 buybacks were $4.67B, but H1’26 FCF was only $7.85B, and H1 dividends alone were $3.97B.** Add buybacks and dividends, and capital return was $8.64B against $7.85B of FCF. That’s a shortfall — and it was covered partly by debt issuance. That’s not a flywheel. That’s borrowing to buy back stock.

## 2. Leverage is not a rounding error — it’s the whole story

You say net leverage of ~2.5x is “exactly management’s target” and investment-grade. Fine. But “at target” means **no cushion**. Gross debt is $162.9B, up $7.8B from December. Net debt is $126.4B. H1’26 debt issuance was $14.0B vs. repayments of $5.4B. That’s net new debt, not just refinancing. And that’s before the $18.9B of capital leases and $18.0B of minority interest. True obligations are higher than the headline debt number.

Interest expense is already up 13.8% YoY — $1.883B in Q2’26 vs. $1.655B a year ago. TTM interest is $7.19B. You say coverage is fine at 7.6x EBITDA/interest. But coverage ratios don’t pay dividends or buybacks. FCF does. And FCF after capex must cover dividends, buybacks, and the rising interest bill. If rates stay higher for longer, refinancing $163B of debt at higher coupons is a direct EPS headwind.

Your FCF coverage math was also wrong. You said TTM FCF of $17.7B vs. TTM dividends of ~$8B plus H1 buybacks of $4.67B annualized to ~$9.3B. But then you added $8B + $4.67B and called it ~$12.7B, leaving a “>25% cushion.” If you actually annualize the buybacks, it’s $8B + $9.3B = $17.3B against $17.7B of FCF. That’s a ~2% cushion, not 25%. And in H1, capital return exceeded FCF. That’s the reality.

## 3. The capex “coiled spring” is a hope, not a catalyst

You frame capex as an asset-building investment. Fair. But AT&T’s capex is ~17.5% of revenue — TTM capex ~$22.2B. There is **no visible peak-capex inflection yet**. You’re asking investors to underwrite a future FCF boom that management hasn’t guided to, while competitors — T-Mobile, Verizon, cable — keep spending and competing.

If AT&T cuts capex too aggressively, it risks network quality and share in a market where pricing power is already thin. And remember: the balance sheet has **negative tangible book of -$88.6B**. Goodwill and intangibles are ~46% of total assets. There is no hard-asset backstop if sentiment turns. You can’t liquidate spectrum and customer relationships at book value in a downturn.

## 4. Competition is not a moat — it’s a grinder

You list scale, spectrum, convergence. But every major carrier has scale and spectrum. Convergence is not unique — Verizon and T-Mobile bundle too. Cable operators like Comcast and Charter are already in mobile. Fixed wireless access is taking broadband share. The US wireless market is saturated; growth comes from taking share or raising prices, not from market expansion.

AT&T’s enterprise wireline business is a declining legacy drag. The fiber build is expensive and faces overbuild risk. 5G is not differentiated enough to support premium pricing. This is a commodity connectivity business. The margin expansion you cite — 23.2% operating margin — is largely cost-cutting, not pricing power. Cost cuts have limits.

## 5. Insider signal is not neutral — it’s a lack of conviction

You say insiders aren’t selling. True. But they’re also **not buying**. Zero open-market purchases in 13 months. For a stock near the middle of its 52-week range, with the bull argument being “it’s cheap,” you’d expect at least some insider accumulation. Instead, executives receive grants and gift shares away. That’s not a vote of confidence. It’s mechanical compensation.

## 6. The asymmetry cuts against the bulls

You say downside is protected by the dividend, low beta, and cheap EV/EBITDA. I say downside is protected only if FCF holds, rates cooperate, capex peaks, and competition doesn’t intensify. That’s a lot of “ifs.”

The bear case requires only one thing to go wrong: FCF disappoints, or rates stay high, or leverage forces buybacks to slow. Any one of those removes the EPS tailwind and exposes the equity as a leveraged claim on a slow-growth business.

At $25.28, **T** is sitting right at its 200-day average of $25.15 and in the lower-middle of its 52-week range. There’s no technical momentum. The market is not mispricing **T** — it’s correctly pricing a company with $162.9B of gross debt, rising interest costs, heavy capex, and a buyback partly funded by borrowing.

So no, Bull, I’m not buying **T** at these levels. I’m avoiding it, and if I owned it, I’d be selling into strength. The cash machine you see is real — but it’s mortgaged.
